# Feature pages

Read this when the job is a marketing page about one product feature: a hero,
a demo, how it works, and a call to action.

## What the page is for

A visitor arrives knowing the product, not the feature. In a few seconds they
should know what the feature does for them, and the demo should prove it
without its caption. Everything else on the page serves that.

## Follow the site's own system

Find an existing feature page on the live site and copy its structure, type,
spacing and order. If the site has a canvas copy of its parts (nav, footer,
buttons), check it against the live site before using it: canvas copies fall
behind, and a stale nav ships quietly. The live site wins.

## The demo

- It shows the feature's payoff: the moment the visitor wants. Build it as an
  animation when the feature is a sequence of actions, which most are.
- Storyboard it with the `animate` skill before building it.
- It is a capture of the product, built from the product's real components.
  Framing made for another medium, such as an email header, a video player
  or a slide title, never appears inside it.
- On a phone, it is the same demo scaled to the column. If it is unreadable at
  that size, change what the demo shows on every width, rather than cropping
  away the part that explains it.

## How it works

- Each step has its own graphic: the moment that step names, settled, not
  caught mid-transition. A step that reuses the demo frame, or another step's
  frame, tells the visitor nothing new. Make the frame the step needs.
- The step graphics share one size and fill their column, so the row reads as
  a set.

## Copy

The headline names the benefit, the lead says how it happens, and the
availability note says where it works. Each is a claim and goes through the
claim check: "where it works" is the one most often wrong.
